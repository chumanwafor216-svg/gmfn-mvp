2. Progression hypothesis (not a compulsory user ladder)
Working model: Unseen → Discoverable → Connected → Transacting → Evidenced → Repeatable → Growing / increasingly self-sufficient.

Progress is non-linear. People start with different strengths and barriers; a service provider with experience but no customers requires different help from a novice with customers but no skills. Avoid publicly labelling people by poverty, hardship, or a universal score. The model must be evaluated using meaningful evidence and user consent, rather than assuming recorded activity equals increased welfare.

Proposed strategic extension: GSN-I-011 in section 21 considers a supported route toward a more secure livelihood and a possible twelve-month programme to test. It connects the existing progression model to actual demand, practical support and sustained outcomes. It is a hypothesis under discussion, not a guaranteed poverty exit or an approved programme.

Scope clarification: GSN-I-012 in section 23 places embedded mentorship within ordinary product design. Human case support is an optional, explicitly resourced extension. A twelve-month evaluation may assess the product's guidance without assuming an intensive staffed programme.

6. Proposed mandatory review questions (draft)
1. Who currently cannot participate, and why? Who receives the main benefit, and how does that relate to the intended beneficiaries?
2. Which obstacle to a member-chosen goal does this decision remove or reduce? What opportunity or assistance is actually available, and which explicit product or service commitment applies if the obstacle remains?
3. What new understanding, action or capability becomes possible?
4. Can someone with limited money, data, literacy, time or equipment realistically use it?
5. What behaviour does the design reward, and could it reward manipulation or privilege?
6. Does it contribute to demand, agreement, fulfilment, credible evidence, useful learning, repeatable opportunity, lasting capability or resilience? For commercial work, what does the participant retain after relevant costs, time and risk? Where future progression is the objective, what useful capacity remains after the immediate need is met?
7. Who pays, who receives value and who bears the risk? Who operates the promised service, and can ordinary delivery function without the founder personally rescuing each case?
8. Can wealthier or better-connected participants capture the benefit or purchase apparent trust? For scarce resources, are eligibility, selection, influence, conflicts and review explicit without breaking existing entitlements?
9. Does this build independent capability or lasting platform dependency?
10. Is it truthful, safe, dignified, lawful, private and sustainable? Does information access match the specific assistance, and do sharing/correction choices preserve both member agency and the integrity of shared records?
11. What evidence would support or disprove the claimed benefit? For commercial claims, distinguish views, requests, agreements, payment evidence and completed outcomes; identify the denominator, time window, missing evidence and effect on first opportunities. For livelihood or poverty claims, define the locally appropriate outcome, costs, sustained observation and GSN's contribution without equating activity with welfare.
12. What is the strongest credible argument against the proposal?
13. Has the member chosen or confirmed the goal and consented to the relevant assistance? 9. GSN-I-004 — Understand before guiding; confirm before committing
Recorded: 27 November 2026.
Status: Candidate principle for discussion; not ratified and not an implementation instruction.
Stimulus: The founder supplied a fragmented spoken advert about repeating a few of another person's words and asked what GSN could learn from FBI negotiation skills. The original video and complete transcript were not independently reviewed.
Related entries: GSN-P-002, GSN-P-007, GSN-P-008, GSN-F-003, GSN-F-005, GSN-F-007 and GSN-I-003.

9.1 What is supported, and what is not
The description is consistent with verbal mirroring or reflection. FBI training material lists reflection of a speaker's recent words alongside paraphrasing, summaries, pauses and open questions as active-listening responses. It also describes role practice with feedback [S1]. Chris Voss describes his version as repeating one to three significant words to invite elaboration, and distinguishes it from paraphrasing [S2].

These sources establish that practitioners teach the technique. They do not establish that people lose control when their words are repeated, that repetition reliably detects lies, that a refusal must turn into agreement, or that it will improve GSN commercial outcomes. The advert's restaurant anecdote is illustrative and unverified. GSN applications below are our proposals, not FBI guidance for marketplaces or validated GSN behaviour.
9.2 Candidate governing principle
Before GSN recommends, judges or commits, it should establish the understanding needed for that decision, make its interpretation easy to correct, and preserve the person's ability to choose or decline.

For a person with little access, the barrier might be customers, transport, tools, unclear terms, money, time or something else. Help should follow the barrier the person actually faces. Communication support should strengthen their ability to explain needs, ask questions, negotiate fair terms and refuse unsuitable arrangements. Fluent language or persuasive confidence must not become a condition of participation or a proxy for trustworthiness.

Philosophy: Respectful understanding and meaningful choice.
Technique: An occasional mirror, open question or brief checked summary.
Product application: Optional guidance, clearer existing forms or editable summaries, subject to separate evaluation.

The philosophy can survive even where repeating words is awkward or inappropriate. Literal mirroring is not a mandatory interface pattern or staff script.

9.4 Illustrative learning card, for discussion only
Situation: A customer says, 'It is too expensive.'
Possible response: 'Too expensive?' spoken with respectful curiosity, followed by a pause. In text, a fuller question may feel more natural: 'Which part of the cost is difficult for you?'
Possible clarification: 'I can afford the repair; it is the delivery charge.'
Lesson: The seller now understands the stated difficulty and can discuss feasible arrangements, such as collection if it is actually available. The seller need not reduce the price or provide delivery they cannot afford.
Boundary: The customer may simply decline. Neither party owes an explanation of private finances. If the total price is beyond their budget, the honest outcome may be no transaction.

An optional practice exercise could ask which response best clarifies the issue while respecting both parties. Its value would be evidence of improved understanding in realistic tasks, not a new badge, trust score or compulsory lesson before trading.
9.5 Strongest objections and design responses
1. Listening can become interrogation. Ask only what is relevant to the requested help or transaction. Offer a direct path where the necessary information is already available.
2. Apparent empathy can be used to manipulate. A person's correction or refusal must be accepted. Do not optimise for disclosures, spending or agreement at any cost.
3. More discussion can increase friction. The current Spotlight objective is a clear handoff to the correct conversation; this insight does not authorise extra steps before that handoff.
4. Smoother communication cannot replace resources. Clarification may reveal missing capital, tools, transport or demand. It does not solve those shortages by itself.
5. A clever slogan can harden into an unnecessary rule. Preserve the purpose of understanding; allow different communication styles and accessibility needs.
9.6 Evidence that would justify adoption
In a small, separately agreed practice or usability exercise, observe whether participants can identify an ambiguous term, ask an appropriate question, correct a mistaken summary and decide freely whether to proceed. Compare the clarity and time required with the existing experience. Look for fewer misunderstandings and unnecessary amendments without added abandonment or pressure. Let participants describe whether the assistance was helpful.

A decision not to proceed because terms are unsuitable can be a good outcome. More messages, longer sessions, more private disclosure, stronger compliance or more purchases are not sufficient evidence of participant benefit. No public measure of hardship, eloquence or psychological susceptibility should be created.
9.7 Proposed future decision question
Does this decision help us understand and serve the participant's actual need, while allowing them to correct us, disagree and say no?

Related incentive question: Are we encouraging clear, voluntary, workable agreements, or merely getting more people to agree?

11. GSN-I-005 — Build capability through achievable, repeated action
Recorded: 27 November 2026.
Status: Candidate refinement of the existing capacity-building philosophy; no ratification or implementation authority.
Stimulus: A fragmented founder-shared spoken excerpt about identity leading behaviour, behaviour confirming identity, consistent alignment and 'automatic success'. The complete recording, speaker and original work have not been established.
Related entries: GSN-P-002, GSN-P-003, GSN-P-007, GSN-F-006, GSN-F-007, GSN-F-010, GSN-I-001 and GSN-I-004.
Decision owner: Founder for eventual adoption. Implementation owner unassigned.
11.1 What the idea contributes
The useful proposition is that a person's sense of what they can become can guide chosen actions, while the experience of carrying out those actions can strengthen confidence and capability. For GSN, this extends the existing principle of turning knowledge into usable capability. It is not a reason to create a separate governing philosophy for every motivational slogan.

Proposed principle:
GSN should help people choose and repeat useful actions that build capability and confidence, recognise credible progress, and make adaptation and restarting possible when circumstances interrupt them.

The relevant progress is defined by the participant's goals and circumstances. Access, dignity and the right to participate must not depend on declaring a positive identity, maintaining a streak or completing a learning programme.
11.2 Source checks and limits
The excerpt resembles the identity-based habits approach James Clear discusses: connect a chosen self-concept to small actions that make it more believable [S4]. This similarity does not identify him as the speaker or establish the excerpt's provenance.

Oyserman and Destin's research-based account treats identity as shaped by context and examines how it affects the interpretation of difficulty. Its educational intervention evidence is not evidence that an identity exercise will improve GSN participants' incomes [S5].

Lally and colleagues studied repeated daily eating, drinking or activity behaviours in consistent contexts. Participants reported increasing automaticity with repetition; missing one opportunity did not materially disrupt formation in that study [S6]. This supports a bounded distinction between learning a routine and guaranteeing economic results. It does not establish a universal habit deadline or prove that success becomes automatic.

Assessment: Retain the practical idea of achievable practice and feedback. Do not adopt guaranteed 'automatic success', inevitable income, a fixed identity, or the claim that all behaviour should stop requiring judgement. People may also start acting before they feel confident; a changed self-description is not a prerequisite.

11.4 Illustrative progression through one practical routine
A hairdresser who is beginning with few customers chooses to become dependable in handling appointments.
1. Choose a practice: Before accepting an appointment, check the requested service, agreed price and realistic time.
2. Make it feasible: Reuse the details already supplied; ask only about material uncertainty.
3. Carry it out: Confirm the arrangement with the customer and explain changes promptly if necessary.
4. Recognise the event accurately: A checked appointment or completed service is a concrete event; a broad claim that the person is always reliable would go beyond the evidence.
5. Reflect and adjust: Notice what helped, what was difficult and whether the routine needs changing.
6. Resume after interruption: Preserve legitimate prior evidence and let the person choose a realistic next action. Do not erase valid transaction problems or responsibilities, but do not turn them into a permanent judgement of character either.
This example proposes a way to support learning. It does not claim the person will get customers, have the required equipment or achieve a particular income simply by adopting the routine.
11.5 Three different meanings of identity
Meaning	What it concerns	GSN consequence
Personal self-concept	How a person sees themselves or wishes to develop.	Keep it self-directed and revisable. Participation does not require disclosure.
Account / verified identity	The person associated with a GS ID and authorised access.	Habit claims or conduct do not replace identity and permission checks.
Evidence of conduct	What can appropriately be said about specified actions or transactions.	Keep evidence contextual, source-labelled and correctable; missing evidence is not evidence of dishonesty.


GSN must not turn 'behaviour confirms identity' into a system that fixes someone's character from limited observations. Strong past evidence also cannot guarantee future performance.
11.6 Strongest challenges
- Circumstance remains real. Useful habits cannot by themselves provide demand, capital, tools, transport, time or reliable connectivity. GSN must keep removing practical barriers rather than attribute exclusion to attitude.
- Consistency is not the same as repeating an ineffective plan. Learning may justify changing an offer, pausing, declining a harmful transaction or pursuing a different market. Persistence should leave room for judgement.
- Setbacks should inform assistance. A missed action does not prove laziness or dishonesty. At the same time, accurate records of obligations and harm should not be concealed.
- Make low-risk routines easier; preserve consequential choices. Reducing repeated effort is useful. Decisions involving material terms, money, privacy and risk still need appropriate understanding and consent.
- Do not turn self-development into surveillance or paid dependency. Prefer participant-chosen goals, permitted data and optional help. No public hardship, discipline or psychological score; no essential access withheld for declining advice.
11.7 Evidence to seek before implementation
In a separately agreed, low-burden trial, test whether a chosen practice helps participants complete a meaningful task, understand its purpose, communicate realistic commitments, recover after interruption and apply the skill with less assistance. Look for practical constraints that prevent the action and whether GSN can reduce them.

Assess outcomes against available opportunity and the person's chosen task. Someone with no incoming customers cannot fairly be judged against an established merchant's transaction count. Useful process improvement and livelihood improvement should be reported separately. A higher login rate, longer streak or increased promotional spend is insufficient evidence of benefit.
11.8 Proposed review question and next debate
Does this decision make a useful capability easier to practise within the participant's circumstances, while allowing them to adapt, pause and begin again?

This adds a practical mechanism to the earlier sequence: understand the actual barrier (GSN-I-004), select a feasible action, practise it, examine the outcome and adapt. It creates no compulsory ladder or new feature instruction.

Questions still open: Which small practices do pilot participants themselves find useful? What event provides an appropriate cue? How can support remain optional and low-cost? How do we recognise progress without letting history become a ceiling on future opportunity?

12.1 Proposed principle
GSN should help people meet present needs while, where feasible, preserving and strengthening their capacity to meet future needs.

This asks what a useful opportunity leaves behind: skill, working tools, accessible records, practical knowledge, appropriate customer relationships, or another resource the person can continue using. It supports the founder's objective of helping people progress from limited access towards sustainable capability. It does not require every interaction to produce an asset or every person to own a business. Stable paid work and improved household security can also represent progress.
12.2 Reading the parable carefully
The apparent distinction is between consuming a resource now, maintaining it for continuing output, and investing in a broader productive activity. These are interpretations of an incomplete story, not verified outcomes or three permanent categories of people.

Eating eggs provides food; selling eggs may produce revenue. Neither alone establishes profit. Production has costs, uncertainties and limits. FAO's egg-production guidance distinguishes sales receipts from costs and identifies feeding, housing, care and production planning as relevant to profitability [S8]. A chicken is therefore not a guaranteed daily income or a perpetual asset. No poultry instructions or investment recommendations are being offered here.
12.3 The central challenge: equal inputs do not guarantee usable opportunity
Someone facing immediate hunger and no means of caring for the bird faces a different choice from someone whose basic needs, space, skills and access to buyers are already covered. GSN must not describe the first person as foolish, morally inferior or incapable of future progress.

The productive question is: What would make preserving or developing the resource a realistic option for this person? The answer may involve an immediate need, a practical constraint, knowledge, demand or support outside GSN's present capacity.

Relevant research provides a reason to examine these constraints together. IPA's account of a six-country Graduation evaluation describes a package combining an income-generating asset with skills, coaching, savings access and basic food or cash support. The latter was intended in part to reduce pressure to sell the asset in an emergency. The package improved several outcomes on average in those settings [S7]. That evidence does not isolate the contribution of any one component, establish that GSN should deliver such a programme, or guarantee a participant's result.

12.5 A practical illustrative application
A hairdresser receives payment for completed work. Market Wisdom could help them think through the costs of that work, materials or tool care needed to continue, immediate household needs and whether anything remains for a chosen improvement or contingency. The person chooses; the system does not allocate or hold their money.

If nothing remains after essential costs and needs, the appropriate response is to examine the real constraint, such as pricing, input costs, available demand or support. It is not to label the person a poor planner. If an improvement is feasible, it should be linked to a demonstrated need: for example, a useful skill or tool for work customers actually request. Buying more equipment merely to appear successful is not the objective.

The same reasoning applies to GSN's product work: preserve useful context, reduce repeated effort and avoid making members pay repeatedly to recover access to work or evidence that should already be usable under the agreed rules.
12.6 Growth and diversification require judgement
Reinvestment may strengthen capacity when there is a feasible use and the person can bear its cost and risk. Keeping resources available for essential needs, maintenance or an unexpected disruption can also support resilience. Diversification is one possible choice; strengthening one workable activity, changing an offer, finding stable paid work or pausing expansion may be more appropriate.

GSN should never make expansion, borrowing, buying assets, foregoing essential consumption or reinvesting all earnings compulsory indicators of progress. Higher revenue can coexist with higher costs and strain. The participant's wellbeing and actual ability to continue matter alongside the size of the activity.
12.7 What to learn from a pilot
Use proportionate, voluntary evidence to explore whether participants gain a useful capability, retain the tools or materials needed to continue, understand relevant costs, reach repeat opportunities if desired, and recover more readily from interruptions. Separate what the platform observes from what a participant reports and what remains unknown.

Do not infer profit from transaction value or self-sufficiency from a first sale. No public poverty, hardship or financial-discipline ranking should be created. Some needs are met outside GSN and some participants may choose to stop using the platform; continued platform dependence is not the objective.

16.3 Evidence and its limits
A World Bank research review found positive average effects of small-business training on sales and profits, with variation across programmes. It also identifies a key limit: if finance, demand or another essential input is the binding constraint, better business practices may not produce growth unless that constraint is addressed. [S15]

A randomised study in Lomé, Togo, found improved profits following personal initiative training among participating microenterprise owners. The sample consisted of applicants with existing firms; eligibility required at least twelve months of operation, and sampling favoured some higher-sales groups. The result does not establish that every excluded person needs mindset training or that GSN learning cards will reproduce that programme's effect. [S16]

These sources support investigating relevant learning and practical constraints together. They do not validate the speech's inevitability claims, establish the speaker's identity, or prove this proposed Market Wisdom design.
16.4 Market Wisdom as practical learning support
Begin with the participant's stated goal and actual barrier. Offer a concise route when useful:
1. Choose a relevant task: What is the person trying to do now?
2. Explain one applicable idea: Use clear language, an accurate source where appropriate and a familiar example.
3. Invite a small practice: Let the participant try a description, calculation, question or plan.
4. Give specific, correctable feedback: Identify the missing term or mistaken assumption. Do not grade the person's character.
5. Apply by choice: Let the participant use the result in an existing workflow. No automatic publication, messaging or acceptance.
6. Reflect and adapt: Ask what helped or remained difficult only when the feedback can improve the next step.
This is a proposed assistance pattern, not a mandatory six-screen journey. A participant who already knows what to do should proceed directly. Help can be brief, optional and available beside the relevant task. The design should work within reasonable limits of literacy, time, language, data and equipment.

Explain a service	Identify scope, inclusions and the information needed for a quote; revise a short example.	A clearer existing Shop offer or Trade Record description, using the person's actual terms.

Understand money received and costs	Work through a fictional example that separates receipts from materials, transport and other relevant costs.	Ability to explain the difference; optional private application to the person's own work. No required financial disclosure or investment prescription.

21.4 The practical operating model

Understand the starting point. Ask what the person wants to achieve, what they can already do and what prevents their next useful step. Ask only what is needed. Resolve the specific obstacle. If the obstacle is a tool, transport, data, accessible equipment, care responsibilities or another practical constraint, identify a suitable supporter or partner. Where an appropriate service is unavailable, state that truthfully and review the plan. Advice should not be used to disguise missing assistance.

Support completion and evidence. Use the shared commercial journey: offer/request, private discussion, agreement, direct-payment reporting, fulfilment and contextual evidence. Preserve the existing distinctions among request, agreed terms, reported payment, seller acknowledgement and completed fulfilment.

Help the person repeat and gain independence. Review what worked, what costs remained, how to obtain repeat work and which capabilities or relationships are becoming reusable. Help someone change direction when the original offer is unsuitable. Success can include work obtained beyond GSN.

The same person might need several rounds of this process. Increasing income does not require everyone to become a business owner; suitable pathways may include employment, apprenticeships, services, contract work or a better trading business. Formal job-placement or training capabilities should be provided through suitable existing partners unless separately designed and approved.

21.6 An illustrative journey
Fictional example, not a GSN success story: A member in Enugu can braid hair, has informal experience and a phone, but has few customers, no premises and limited tools.

The initial task is to establish a feasible offer and demand. A coordinator might help identify an appropriate local customer need or a suitable arrangement with an existing salon. Availability and consent must be real; the example assumes such a partner is actually secured.

Market Wisdom could help the member make a clear offer, understand materials and travel costs, practise a customer response and agree what will be delivered. Spotlight and a simple Shop presence could present the offer. The existing transaction process could record the agreement and what followed.

After the first job, the useful question is what remains: earnings after costs, useful experience, a customer willing to return, and evidence that can support another opportunity. If the next obstacle is access to a chair or tools, a relevant arrangement matters more than another motivational lesson.

Over time, the member might obtain repeat customers, a paid role, a better salon arrangement or an independent business. Owning a salon is not the compulsory definition of success. A more dependable livelihood with greater choice can itself be meaningful progress.

The example depends on real demand, practical delivery and appropriate support. Software coordinates those ingredients; it does not establish their existence.
21.7 The psychology of the product
The intended experience should communicate: “I have something to contribute; I can understand my next step; appropriate help is reachable; my progress is recognised accurately; I can restart or change direction.”

Use a few practical questions and allow the person to correct the interpretation. Avoid public hardship labels, moral judgements, compulsory motivational tasks and rankings of personal worth.

A proposed member view could show:
- Your goal: what the member has chosen.
- Next useful step: one manageable action.
- Why it matters: the relevant opportunity or obstacle.
- Available help: a real person, service, lesson or tool, with accurate availability.
- Progress: completed work and useful learning supported by appropriate evidence.
- Change the plan: correct the assumptions, request help, pause or choose another route.
This is conceptual presentation guidance, not an instruction to add six new cards to the frozen Dashboard. It could be tested in an existing journey or an opt-in support programme.

Market Wisdom should preserve its full mentoring role: encouragement, examples, reflection and practical advice. A daily nugget can support that relationship, while a real obstacle requires a response matched to it. Missed activity should prompt a respectful check when useful, rather than a conclusion about laziness or dishonesty.

The goal is increasing competence and agency. A person who eventually needs less prompting may represent success. Learning time, family responsibilities, rest and legitimate pauses should not automatically be treated as lack of commitment.
21.8 Technology and the minimum information needed
At an appropriate future stage, the product might need a small, permissioned record of:
- The member's chosen goal and current plan.
- The next useful action and its context.
- The current obstacle, as stated or confirmed by the member.
- Relevant opportunities and practical support actually available.
- An accountable supporter where a support programme provides one.
- The result, its evidence and any change of plan.
This can begin with a tightly controlled manual pilot using existing records and tools. Do not commission a parallel progression database or AI engine before establishing which missing capability matters.

Reuse the existing identity, Shop, commercial conversation, Trade Record and evidence infrastructure. Keep support information separate from public reputation and advertising allocation. Use permitted activity for its stated purpose; members should be able to correct mistaken conclusions.

The mission also exposes access questions: can someone with limited data or a modest phone participate, and what happens when an otherwise eligible person lacks the banking access assumed by the reported seller-payment journey? The latter requires an actual supported route or an appropriate partner; do not improvise payments through another person's identity or claim a payment capability that does not exist.

Relevant technical investment should reduce a known obstacle: reliable uploads, simple language, recoverable drafts, easy return to the same conversation, affordable data use, permissions and appropriate notification delivery. Adding an AI model is useful only where it makes the chosen task more achievable.

21.11 Financing the support and preparing operations
A credible programme needs a costed delivery model covering platform operation, coordinator/mentor time, practical support, partner services, participant access costs where subsidised, and evaluation.

Possible funding sources include useful paid services for established participants, community or institutional contracts, defined sponsorship and voluntary paid growth services. These are options to test; no payer or budget is confirmed by this discussion.

Define what basic participation provides, which help is funded for selected participants, which services carry a fee, and what happens when support ends. Members should understand the arrangement before committing time or money. Sponsor influence should not purchase false trust, hidden priority or members' private hardship information.

Practical readiness requires actual buyers, capable coordinators, accessible support and a manageable participant count. A coordinator should know whom they are helping, what assistance is available, and what to do when progress stalls. Claims about personalised support must match staffing and response capacity.

The founder should not become the permanent answer to every stalled case. Document common decisions and escalation routes, test them, and empower trained people to handle ordinary situations within clear boundaries. This supports the earlier aim of a philosophy that lets future staff make sound decisions without seeking approval for every step.
21.12 What would count as success
Separate four levels of evidence:
1. Activity: a member registered, read a nugget or published an offer.
2. Useful commercial progress: a relevant response, agreed task, completed work or repeat customer.
3. Livelihood improvement: better net earnings, more dependable opportunities, greater ability to meet essential needs and increased capability or resilience.
4. GSN's contribution: evidence that the programme helped produce the improvement rather than merely coinciding with it.
For a consenting pilot, define a small baseline and outcome set before launch. Include direct costs and time, not only sales totals. Record lost contact, withdrawal, non-completion and harms alongside successes. An appropriate local household-level measure is needed for a poverty claim; one seller's transaction count cannot supply it.

GSN's current reported payment process does not independently verify external bank transfers. Seller acknowledgement, member-reported income and independently verified evidence should remain distinguishable.

Proposed practical measures include time to a relevant opportunity, time to first paid work, repeat paid work, net earnings after direct costs, reliability of earnings, meaningful access to skills/tools, ability to meet locally relevant essential commitments, and participant choice or reduced dependence. Collect only what is useful and voluntarily agreed; intrusive household disclosure should not be a condition of ordinary platform access.

Track the whole defined cohort with denominators and observation windows. Before-and-after improvement can be informative but does not by itself prove causation. A research partner could help choose a feasible comparison or phased evaluation, with appropriate participant protections. No experimental allocation is authorised here.

Commercial sustainability also needs measurement: full support cost, who pays it and whether an affordable model can maintain the benefit. A high transaction total with poor member earnings or an unfinanceable support burden is incomplete success.
21.13 The strongest challenges
Demand may be the limiting resource. More training, adverts and members cannot guarantee enough customers. The next decisive investment may be recruiting buyers and arranging access to an existing market.

Some obstacles need material or specialist support. GSN should know when guidance reaches its limit and connect suitable assistance where it exists. Severe immediate deprivation may require stabilisation before earning plans become usable.

Current conversion data can favour established sellers. Review whether improvement helps people gain a first credible opportunity while honouring paid exposure. Avoid silently substituting a wealth or reputation advantage for evidence of potential.

Local networks can contain exclusion as well as help. Keep appropriate review and alternative routes, so a single gatekeeper does not control a member's future.

An apparent success can be economically weak. More work may leave little after costs or create excessive burdens. Assess the member's outcome.

A one-year target can distort incentives. Selecting only easy cases or suppressing dropouts would undermine the mission. Publish who the programme served, who it could not help and why.

These are design and evaluation questions, not reasons to abandon the ambition.
21.14 A bounded next experiment to debate
A useful initial test would focus on one participant segment, one place, a small number of feasible earning pathways and a participant count matched to actual support capacity. Enugu could be considered because of the founder's existing field context; that is a proposed choice, not a new approved cohort.

Before recruitment, settle:
- The participant group and its main constraints, including people starting with limited resources.
- Real buyers or other demand sources and feasible delivery requirements.
- The practical help available, its provider and budget.
- The coordinator/mentor responsibilities and escalation path.
- The actual product and payment-access capabilities needed.

- Transparent eligibility and reporting of people who do not progress.
Begin with the smallest supported journey, learn where it fails, then implement the recurring missing capability. Continue the approved Spotlight work in parallel; this discussion does not replace its current scope or authorise deployments.
